01 — The problem

Written for the regulator. Read by a parent.

Allied health websites tend to read like compliance documents: service lists, funding categories, the language of the sector talking to itself. The person actually reading is almost never in that frame of mind. They are a parent who has been told to get an assessment, is not sure what that means, and is quietly worried.

A new practice has the additional problem of having no track record to point at. Everything has to be carried by how it sounds.

Services described in the words a parent would use

02 — The idea

Lead with the people and the place, not the service list.

Therapy for little ones from newborn to nine, delivered in your own home, at day care or at school. That is the difference, so it is the first thing on the page, not the fourth item under a heading called “Our Services”.

Daphne and Sallina are introduced by name and photograph, early, because a parent handing over their child wants to know who that is. The qualifications are all there. They are simply not the opening argument.

Faces before credentials

03 — The detail

Booking is the end of reading, not a separate errand.

The moment a parent decides is the moment they should be able to act. Booking sits at the natural end of the page rather than behind a “Contact” link, and it shows actual available times instead of a form asking someone to get in touch.

Around it: a referral a GP or an educator can complete without ringing, published fees, an FAQ that answers the awkward questions, and Grove Notes, somewhere for the practice to be useful in public rather than only in appointments.

Real times, at the point of deciding

04 — Where it is read

One-handed, late, tired.

Parents research at night, on a phone, usually with something else going on. The mobile layout is not the desktop one folded up: the referral and the phone number stay within reach, and the type is set to be read by someone who is not at their best.
